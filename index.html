Ranking Signal Analysis

Does AI-driven optimization change search position — or just find pages that were already winning?

A signal-level study of ~106,000 content items across 38 clients, testing whether optimization status, content length, and keyword competition are associated with search ranking — and whether the associations survive obvious confounds.

Lane — Ranking Signal Analysis Rows analyzed — 105,810 Clients — 38 of 104 Validation — 5-fold grouped CV
01 — Abstract

Question. Which observable content and keyword signals are associated with organic search position, and do those associations survive checks for obvious confounds? Method. Content-level signals from a dimension table were joined to 90-day query performance, aggregated to one row per content item, and tested first with correlation and group comparisons, then with a linear regression validated by client-grouped 5-fold cross-validation against a mean-prediction baseline. Result. AI-optimization status was the strongest association with position (coefficient −14.8), a gap that held after checking backlinks, keyword competition, and content age, and that coincided with a clear pattern: non-optimized content decayed in position as it aged, while optimized content did not. Content length and category count showed statistically detectable but practically negligible effects. The model modestly outperformed a baseline (MAE 23.2 vs 24.1), and one relationship — search intent — reversed direction between the raw and multivariate view, which is reported as a finding rather than smoothed over.

02 — Introduction

The decision this supports

Content teams have a limited optimization budget and a large backlog of published pages. The practical question behind this analysis is simple: if a page could be optimized, is that likely to matter, and how does that likely effect compare to the effect of factors outside the content team's control — like how competitive the target keyword already is? A signal-level view, rather than a single black-box score, lets a team see which levers are worth pulling and which are just context to plan around.

This is deliberately scoped as an associational study, not a causal one. No signal here is manipulated experimentally; the goal is to rank candidate signals by strength and credibility of evidence, and to be explicit about which associations are more likely to reflect a real effect versus which pages happened to get selected for optimization in the first place.

03 — Data

Warehouse release & scope

This analysis uses four tables from the FlyRank internship warehouse release: dim_content (one row per content item — length, category, optimization, and keyword-economics fields), dim_clients (client-level access flags), and fact_content_query_90d (one row per client–content–query, with 90-day and last/prev-30-day position and click windows). The full daily fact table was reviewed for schema but not used in the final model, in favor of the pre-aggregated 90-day query fact, which better matched the content-level grain this lane calls for.

Filters applied

  • Content restricted to is_published = true, is_deleted = false
  • Clients restricted to is_active = true, has_gsc_access = true, so position data reflects real search-console visibility
  • Content items with zero 90-day impressions excluded (no position signal to measure)
  • Rows missing the target (weighted_position_last30) or content age excluded from modeling — 13,975 rows (11.7%), reported rather than imputed

After filtering, 105,810 content items across 38 clients remained for modeling — a meaningful narrowing from the 104 clients in the warehouse, driven mostly by the GSC-access requirement. This is a real scope limitation: findings describe clients with active search-console integration, not the full client base.

04 — Methodology

Assumptions, features, and validation design

Outcome definition

The primary outcome is an impressions-weighted average position across a content item's queries in the most recent 30-day window (weighted_position_last30), where a lower value is a better rank. A secondary movement metric (position_movement) compares the prior and most recent 30-day windows.

Feature set

Five candidate signals were carried into the model after exploratory review: is_ai_optimized (whether model_used is populated), content_age_days, word_count, category_count, main_intent, and competition_level. char_count was dropped after modeling — it was nearly collinear with word_count and produced an unstable, sign-flipped coefficient when both were included.

Missing-data handling

Roughly a quarter of content items have no populated word_count/char_count/optimization model — this is structural, not random: content that hasn't entered the optimization pipeline doesn't have those fields. Rather than impute a mean, missing length values were set to zero and is_ai_optimized was left to carry that information as its own signal. main_intent and competition_level nulls were assigned an explicit UNKNOWN category rather than silently dropped.

Validation design

Content items are grouped under 38 clients; a random row-level split would let a client's site-wide characteristics leak between train and test. All validation instead used GroupKFold cross-validation (5 folds) grouped by client_hash_id, so no client's content ever appears in both a fold's training and test set. Given the small number of groups (38 clients), a single train/test split was judged too noisy to trust on its own.

Leakage checks performed

  • Verified backlinks, search_volume, and competition medians did not differ meaningfully between optimized and non-optimized content, ruling those out as confounds for the optimization effect
  • Compared the optimization effect within content-age quartiles rather than only in aggregate, to check whether it was driven by non-optimized content simply being newer
  • Reviewed optimization_eligible_date spread (57–617 days from creation, not a fixed offset) to assess whether optimization timing looked schedule-driven or performance-triggered — inconclusive, and reported as an open limitation rather than resolved
05 — Results

Model vs. baseline, and what drives the prediction

A linear regression using the five features above was compared against a baseline that predicts the training-fold mean position for every row, evaluated identically across all 5 grouped folds.

The model improves on baseline by ~3.4% — modest, and reported as such. Fold-to-fold variance was low (21.3–24.9 MAE across folds), which is what makes this a trustworthy, if unspectacular, number rather than an artifact of one lucky split.

Holds up under checking

AI-optimization status is the strongest signal in the model (coefficient −14.8). Non-optimized content shows a clear decay pattern — median position worsens from ~16 to ~53 as content ages past 155 days — while optimized content holds steady in the low-to-high 20s regardless of age. This gap persisted after checking backlinks and keyword competition as possible confounds.

Reversed under multivariate control

Search intent's relationship with position flips once other signals are held constant. In a raw group comparison, informational content had the best median position (23.1) of any intent category. In the full model, holding optimization status and competition level constant, informational content shows a worse position than commercial content. This mild Simpson's-paradox pattern suggests the raw ordering was likely driven by informational content getting optimized more often or facing easier keyword competition — not an intrinsic advantage of informational content itself. Flagged for further investigation rather than resolved here.

Content length signals (word_count, category_count) were statistically distinguishable from zero (Spearman p < 0.0001 given the large sample) but practically small — under one position of predicted effect per unit change, and not a priority lever on this evidence.

06 — Limitations & honest framing

What this analysis can and can't claim

Every relationship reported here is observed and directional, not causal. In particular:

  • Optimization is not randomly assigned. Eligibility timing varies from 57 to 617 days after content creation and does not look like a fixed schedule; this analysis could not determine from the available fields whether pages are selected for optimization based on early performance, which would inflate the apparent effect.
  • Only content items with active Google Search Console access are represented — findings should not be generalized to the full, unfiltered client base without re-checking on that population.
  • CTR and impressions were deliberately excluded from the signal list despite strong correlation with position (r ≈ −0.33 to −0.44), because that relationship is close to definitional in organic search rather than an independent content signal.
  • The model explains a modest share of variance in position; most of the variation in ranking is driven by factors outside this signal set (backlink profiles, content quality dimensions not captured here, competitor behavior).

Recommendations below are worded as decision-support, not guarantees.
