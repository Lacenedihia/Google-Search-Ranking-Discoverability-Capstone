0.Abstract

Research Question: Which content signals are associated with search ranking position, and how does AI-driven optimization associate with that position?

Methodology: I analyzed 90-day Google Search Console performance data for 105,810 published content items from 38 FlyRank clients (out of 104 clients in the warehouse). A linear regression models the impressions-weighted average position over the last 30 days as a function of AI optimization status, content age, structural features and keyword competitiveness. Evaluation uses 5-fold cross-validation grouped by client to prevent leakage.

Key Finding: AI-optimized content ranks substantially better than non-optimized content: median position 18.4 vs. 48.3. After adjusting for content age, word count, category count, search intent and keyword competition, the optimization coefficient is −14.8 positions. The gap is also visible within every content-age quartile and does not disappear when content with and without backlinks is compared separately. Non-optimized content shows a much steeper rank decline with age.

Predictive power: The model reduces the grouped-CV mean absolute error only slightly, from 24.06 to 23.23 positions (about 3.5%). The model is therefore used to interpret associations, not to predict the position of an individual page.

Limitations & Framing: Optimization eligibility is non-random and the selection logic is not observed. The result is strong directional evidence, not a proven causal effect.

1.Problem Framing

The Business Question

Search visibility drives organic traffic. The central question is:

Which content signals are associated with ranking position, and can we quantify the association between AI-driven optimization and search position?

Why This Matters

  • Content prioritization: With thousands of pages, teams cannot optimize everything.
  • AI optimization ROI: Many clients apply AI-driven optimization; measuring its observable association with rankings informs resource allocation.
  • Automation path: Knowing which signals matter supports automated scoring and recommendations.

Methodological Stance

This is a predictive and descriptive modeling problem, not a causal inference task. Optimization was not randomly assigned, so the analysis measures associations while checking the confounders that can be observed, and uses observed / directional / decision-support language throughout.

Data Quality & Rows Dropped

Rows dropped for modeling: 13,975 of 119,785 rows (11.7%) have no weighted_position_last30 (no impressions in the last 30 days) or no content age. 38 clients remain in the model.

Structural missingness: model_used is populated for 74.8% of rows and word_count for 72.9%. These fields are empty for non-optimized content by design. is_ai_optimized is defined as model_used IS NOT NULL. Missing word_count is filled with 0 so that is_ai_optimized carries the information, which also means word_count cannot be interpreted separately from the optimization flag. Missing main_intent (1,502 rows) and competition_level are filled with "UNKNOWN".

Public Safety Compliance

  • No client names or domains: everything is anonymized at client_hash_id level.
  • No private queries: query data is aggregated per content item.
  • No algorithmic claims: the analysis does not model Google's algorithm, only FlyRank clients' outcomes on their own properties.
  • No raw exports: only summary statistics and model outputs.

Confounder Checks

  • Age: optimized content is more recent (median creation date January 2026 vs. July 2025), so age is a real confounder. Within each of the four age quartiles, optimized content still ranks better (Figure 3).
  • Backlinks: the median number of backlinks is 0 in both groups, so backlinks cannot explain the gap. Splitting by "has backlinks / no backlinks", the gap persists (median 48.1 vs. 18.4 without backlinks; 50.8 vs. 18.2 with backlinks). Backlinks are not a model feature.
  • Competition and search volume: median search volume is 10 in both groups and the median competition score is similar (0.00 for optimized, 0.03 for non-optimized). Competition level is also a model feature.

These checks do not eliminate confounding (observational data, not an experiment), but the optimization association is not explained by the observable variables above.

Optimized content: 13.3, then 20.1, 21.8 and 21.5.

Both groups decline with age, but the decline is much larger for non-optimized content. Note that this comes from median comparisons by age quartile; the linear model has no age × optimization interaction term.

Intent and Competition

Raw medians: informational content ranks better (23.1) than commercial (28.6) and transactional (25.7); LOW-competition keywords rank at a median of 22.6, against 34.3 (HIGH) and 36.3 (MEDIUM). The "navigational" intent has only 160 rows and should not be interpreted.

Model Architecture

Model type

Linear regression (OLS)

Chosen for interpretability: each coefficient is an adjusted association with ranking position.

Preprocessing

ColumnTransformer pipeline

Numeric features: StandardScaler. Categorical: OneHotEncoder (drop='first'), so coefficients are relative to a reference category (competition: HIGH; intent: UNKNOWN).

Train/Test Design: Grouped by Client

A random row split would leak: content from the same client shares site-level effects (niche, design, SEO maturity). All evaluation therefore keeps each client entirely in either train or test.

A first single grouped split (GroupShuffleSplit, 20% test, seed 42) gave 79,716 training rows (30 clients) and 26,094 test rows (8 clients): baseline MAE 25.32, model MAE 23.71. With only 8 test clients, that result depends strongly on which clients land in the test set, so the final evaluation uses 5-fold GroupKFold.

6.Interpretation & Honest Framing

What the data supports

  • AI-optimized content has a median position of 18.4 vs. 48.3 for non-optimized content.
  • The advantage persists within each content-age quartile and for content with or without backlinks.
  • In a linear model with grouped cross-validation, optimization is the largest coefficient (−14.8 positions).
  • Non-optimized content declines more steeply with age than optimized content.

What the data does not support

  • "AI optimization causes better rankings."
  • "Optimizing a page will improve it by 15 positions."
  • "This shows how Google's algorithm treats optimized content."
  • "The model predicts a page's position accurately" (MAE 23.2 vs. 24.1 baseline).

Why causality cannot be claimed

  1. Selection timing: optimization eligibility varies from 57 to 617 days after creation (mean about 300 days), and the selection rule is unobserved.
  2. Unobserved selection: content considered but not optimized cannot be seen, so treatment and control groups may differ in unmeasured ways.
  3. Maintenance confounding: optimized content may also receive refreshes or links that are not recorded.
  4. Few clients: only 38 clients remain in the model, so client-level effects can still shape the results.
"Content with AI-driven optimization shows better search position than non-optimized content, and this association holds within content-age strata and after adjustment for competition and intent. Because eligibility for optimization is non-random and its trigger logic is not observable, this is strong directional evidence supporting the prioritization of optimization workflows, not a proven causal effect."

7.Recommendations

Supported by this analysis

1. Prioritize AI-optimization coverage

Evidence: the strongest association in the model (−14.8 positions), robust across age and backlink checks. Confidence: directional, not causal.

2. Segment strategy by keyword competition

Evidence: LOW-competition keywords rank about 6 positions better than HIGH after adjustment. Competition is a property of the keyword, not a content lever, so it is useful for setting expectations and choosing targets.

3. Treat word count and category count as low-priority levers

Evidence: coefficients below 1 position per standard deviation.

4. Investigate intent before acting on it

Evidence: raw medians favor informational content, but model coefficients are relative to a filled-in "UNKNOWN" group, so this signal needs a cleaner reference category before any decision.

Suggestions (not derived from this data)

  • Refresh review for older content: content age is associated with worse positions; a refresh process could be tested.
  • Feedback loop: track rank change at 30, 60 and 90 days after optimization, compared with a control group, to move from association toward causal evidence.

9.Limitations & Future Work

Known Limitations

Observational data

No random assignment; selection into optimization is not observed.

Few clients

Only 38 of 104 clients remain after filters and missing-value drops; client-level effects can still matter.

Structural missingness

Word count is empty for non-optimized content, so it is tied to the optimization flag.

Low predictive power

MAE improves by only about 3.5% over the baseline. The linear model also assumes one common effect for all content, with no interaction between age and optimization.

90-day window

Long-term effects are not visible, and the 18% of rows without impressions in the last 30 days are not modeled.

No engagement signals

CTR is a consequence of position, so it was kept out of the model.

Future Work

  • Causal design: propensity-score matching or instrumental variables, using the eligibility date; ideally a randomized trial.
  • Richer models: an age × optimization interaction, gradient-boosted trees as a comparison, and a mixed-effects model with a random effect per client.
  • Segmentation: separate models by competition level and intent.
  • Longer windows and maintenance signals: link acquisition and refresh frequency over 12 months.
